Do You Need a Permit for Plumbing Work in Tempe? A Real Answer

By the Tempe 24/7 Plumbing team · Updated 2026-09-13

Permit requirements are one of the most commonly misunderstood parts of a plumbing project, and the honest answer is that it depends on exactly what the work involves. According to the City of Tempe's Building Safety Division, permits are required for plumbing work that changes the system rather than simply repairing it like-for-like.

What Typically Needs a Permit

Water heater replacements that change the gas line, venting, or electrical connection typically need a permit, even though a straightforward like-for-like swap sometimes doesn't, depending on what's actually changing. Whole-home repiping, new fixture installations that add plumbing where none existed, and backflow prevention assembly installations all typically require a permit and inspection.

Sewer line replacement, particularly work that affects the connection to the city's main line, is another category that requires permitting and coordination with the city. For properties in West Mesa or our other nearby service areas, the permit is issued by that city's own building department rather than Tempe's, since permitting runs through each local jurisdiction separately.

What Usually Doesn't Need One

Routine repairs, fixing a leak, unclogging a drain, replacing a faucet cartridge, or repairing an existing fixture without changing the system, generally don't require a permit. The distinction the City draws is between repair and alteration: fixing what's there differently than replacing it with something that changes the system's configuration.

Why This Matters Beyond Just Following the Rules

Unpermitted work that should have been permitted can become a real problem at resale, when a home inspection or the buyer's own research reveals work that was never inspected. It can also affect insurance claims if a problem later traces back to unpermitted work. Getting this right the first time is meaningfully cheaper than untangling it years later.

When we handle work that requires a permit, whether that's a water heater replacement with venting changes or a full repipe, pulling the permit and scheduling inspection is part of the job, not an extra step you have to manage separately.

What the Permit and Inspection Process Actually Looks Like

Once a permit is pulled, the work itself proceeds the same way it would without one, the difference is that a City inspector reviews the completed work against code before it's considered finished. For most residential plumbing permits, this means one inspection visit scheduled after the work is done but before any walls or flooring are closed up over new piping.

If the inspection reveals an issue, it needs to be corrected and re-inspected before the permit closes out. This is exactly why permitted work tends to be more reliable long-term: it's been checked by someone with no stake in cutting corners.

A Real Example: Water Heater Replacement

A straightforward like-for-like tank swap, same fuel type, same capacity, same location, sometimes doesn't require a permit in Tempe. The moment that job changes to a different fuel type, relocates the unit, or alters the venting or gas line sizing, it crosses into permit-required territory. This is exactly the kind of detail that's easy to miss if you're not familiar with the distinction, and it's why we confirm permit status before starting rather than assuming based on the general category of work.

How This Compares Across Our Service Area

Permit requirements themselves come from the City of Tempe specifically, but the underlying logic, repair versus alteration, applies the same way in neighboring jurisdictions too. A property in West Mesa follows Mesa's own building department process, while one in Ahwatukee falls under Phoenix's jurisdiction, even though the actual plumbing work looks identical to a job done in Tempe proper. We confirm the correct jurisdiction and process for each specific address rather than assuming Tempe's rules apply everywhere we work.

What Happens If a Buyer's Inspector Finds Unpermitted Work

During a home sale, an inspector or appraiser sometimes flags plumbing work that looks recent but has no permit record on file with the city. This doesn't automatically kill a sale, but it typically becomes a negotiation point, and in some cases a lender may require it be brought into compliance before closing. Sellers are often better served getting ahead of this by having any questionable past work assessed before listing, rather than being surprised by it during a buyer's inspection.

How to Check Whether Past Work Was Permitted

Most cities, including Tempe, maintain public permit records that can be searched by address. Before assuming past work on a home you own or are buying was done properly, a quick permit history search is worth the few minutes it takes, especially for anything involving a water heater, sewer line, or repiping that would reasonably have needed one. If a permit record is missing for work that should have had one, that's worth raising directly rather than assuming it was simply an oversight in record-keeping.

The Bottom Line

When in doubt about whether a specific job needs a permit, ask before the work starts rather than after. It's a quick question, and getting it wrong in either direction, skipping a required permit or paying for one that wasn't needed, costs more to sort out later than confirming it upfront. A five-minute question at the start of a project is always cheaper than untangling a compliance issue after the fact, whether that's at resale, during an insurance claim, or simply when a future contractor asks for records that were never created. When we quote a job, confirming permit status is one of the first things we check, not an afterthought added once work is already underway.
